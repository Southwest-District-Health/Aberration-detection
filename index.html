What a signal means: the number of cases reported that week is statistically higher than the baseline the model expects for that condition. It is worth a closer look, not a conclusion.

What it does not mean: it does not mean there is a confirmed outbreak, that cases are linked, or that the data are wrong. A signal can reflect a small absolute change in a low-count condition, a reporting delay or catch-up, or ordinary random variation. Signals should prompt review of case details, shared exposures, and data quality.

How the detection works

Two ways to flag an unusual week

Each condition's weekly case counts are screened for weeks that look statistically higher than expected. What counts as expected depends on whether a condition has a seasonal pattern, so one of two methods is chosen automatically for each condition.

  • Farrington Flexible, for conditions with a real seasonal pattern such as RSV peaking in winter. It builds its baseline from the same calendar week in each of the past several years, so it knows what normal looks like for that time of year.
  • GLR-NB (negative-binomial generalized likelihood ratio), for conditions that stay roughly flat year-round or have very low, sparse counts. It fits one flat baseline rate from several years of history, then flags weeks that statistically exceed it.

Farrington needs enough history at each specific calendar week to fit a stable model, and for flat or sparse conditions there often isn't enough. GLR-NB avoids that by not requiring week-matched history, but it can't represent a seasonal curve, so it isn't a good substitute for genuinely seasonal diseases. The choice is made by comparing a flat baseline with a seasonal one for each condition's history and using whichever fits significantly better.

Conditions marked Insufficient data have too few cases or years to model reliably. Their counts are shown, but no weeks are flagged.
